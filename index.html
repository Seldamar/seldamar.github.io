About

I am an applied economist working on health economics, public finance, and inclusive growth. I am a PhD candidate at ISEG, Universidade de Lisboa, and I will be on the 2026–27 academic job market.

My dissertation, Inclusive Growth and Health Expenditures, asks how the way countries finance health care shapes the joint dynamics of growth, inequality, and poverty. Using cross-country panels and structural and dynamic methods, I show that public health spending strengthens the redistributive reach of growth and buffers economies against pandemic shocks, while heavy reliance on out-of-pocket payments does the opposite. My supervisor is Prof. João Jalles, and my work is supported by a 2025 FCT Doctoral Scholarship.

Before returning to my doctorate I spent three years as a data analyst in industry, most recently as a Senior Marketing Analyst at Cognizant, which sharpened my applied data skills and my sense of what evidence policymakers and practitioners can actually use.

Job Market Paper

Pandemics, Inequality, and the Public Health Expenditure Buffer: Evidence from Five Epidemics and COVID-19

REM Working Paper 0412/2026March 2026

Do countries that invest in public health before a pandemic suffer less economic damage when one arrives? We estimate the dynamic effects of pandemic shocks on GDP per capita, the disposable-income Gini, and household consumption using local projections on 138 countries over 2000 to 2019, with shocks weighted by within-episode severity and the sample extended to 2023 for COVID-19. Three results emerge. First, pandemics durably widen the income distribution: the Gini rises by half a point within five years, in every specification we examine, with the increase concentrated in emerging markets. Output and consumption losses are conditional, appearing when outbreaks are severe and once financial crisis variation is removed. Second, the finding that high-spending countries fare worse is an artifact of the 2009 overlap between H1N1 and the global financial crisis; correcting it reverses the sign, but the pre-COVID episodes are too few for a significant result. Third, COVID-19 provides the test the earlier episodes cannot: countries with low predetermined health spending lost 3.4 to 3.5 percent of GDP, while high-spending countries show no significant loss. This protection is specific to public health spending and does not appear for private spending. It also has a limit: inequality rises no matter how much countries spend.

Public versus Private Health Spending in the Growth–Inequality–Poverty Nexus

REM Working Paper 0411/2026March 2026

For the distribution of income, how a country finances health care may matter more than how much it spends. This paper estimates economic growth, income inequality, and household welfare as a simultaneous three-equation system, using three-stage least squares with country fixed effects on a panel of 94 countries over 2000–2023, and separates public from private health expenditure throughout. Identification comes from within-country variation, which removes the permanent differences between countries that confound cross-country comparisons. Public and private health spending are both associated with higher output, with similar elasticities, but they diverge on distribution: public spending is associated with lower income inequality and a lower poverty headcount, while private, largely out-of-pocket spending shows no association with inequality and is associated with higher poverty. These distributional associations are concentrated in emerging-market and low-income economies, where public financing displaces out-of-pocket payments, and are absent in advanced economies with near-universal coverage. Several associations reverse sign in cross-country estimation, indicating that cross-sectional designs can misattribute permanent country differences to health policy. The composition of health financing, not only its level, shapes distributional outcomes.

Out-of-Pocket Health Spending and Income Inequality in the United States: Threshold Effects and Structural Change

Dissertation essay2026

Income inequality across US states has risen to historic highs, and the channels driving it remain incompletely understood. This paper examines a channel that has received limited empirical attention: the financing of health care through out-of-pocket (OOP) payments. Using a balanced panel of 51 US states over 2004–2019, we apply the Hansen (1999) fixed-effect panel threshold model with OOP burden measured relative to household capacity to pay (CTP) and ask whether the relationship between OOP burden and inequality is regime-dependent. We document two complementary findings. First, the marginal inequality effect of OOP burden exhibits a statistically significant regime shift at 11.84% of CTP, a level well below the conventional 40% catastrophic-spending threshold, with an effect approximately 17% larger above this level than below. Second, the strength of this channel has not been stable over time: the marginal inequality effect of OOP burden fell by roughly half around 2009, a shift that appears under both burden measures and is better characterized as a discrete break than a gradual decline. We find no additional shift at the 2014 implementation of the Affordable Care Act and no differential decline in Medicaid-expansion states, indicating that the weakening of the channel predates the ACA. These results indicate that the distributional consequences of OOP financing begin to intensify well within the moderate, non-catastrophic burden, and that the channel is time-varying in ways that existing policy-attribution narratives do not capture.

Master's dissertation

Military Expenditure and Economic Growth in the United States, 1947–2018: An Empirical Analysis

Università degli Studi di Milano2020

Quarterly US data from 1947 to 2018 are used to study the two-way relationship between defense spending and GDP growth. VAR and SVAR estimates with Granger causality tests support the growth-led hypothesis: defense spending responds to positive growth shocks, but not the reverse. An ARDL specification then isolates the effect of defense spending on growth without the identification restrictions an SVAR requires.
